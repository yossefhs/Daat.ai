Siman 214 — The custom that becomes a prohibition (לעבור על מנהג צריך התרה)

1. The axiom: it is not the word that binds, it is knowing conduct

The axiom. A permitted thing, known to be permitted, from which one has taken to abstaining while intending from the outset that it be forever, is treated as a vow. Two conditions, then, and they are in the text: the knowledge, and the intention to persist.
« דברים המותרים והיודעים בהם שהם מותרים נהגו בהם איסור הוי כאילו קבלו עליהם בנדר ואסור להתירם בהם » (שו״ע יו״ד רי״ד:א)
« אם בשעה שהתחיל לנהוג היה דעתו לנהוג כן לעולם ונהג כן אפילו פעם אחת צריך התרה » (שו״ע יו״ד רי״ד:א)
« נהגו בהן איסור. מחמת סייג וגדר ופרישות: » (ש״ך יו״ד רי״ד ס״ק א)
Three consequences. (1) Three men are needed to be released, opening with regret. (2) A single occasion suffices to bind, if the intention was to persist. (3) One who abstained by error accepted nothing at all.

2. The three reflex questions

1. Did he know the thing to be permitted? No — it was an error, there is no vow. Yes — next question.
▼
2. Did he intend from the outset that it be forever? No — nothing binds him. Yes — even a single occasion suffices.
▼
3. What custom is this? His own — three men and regret. His town’s — seif 2. All Israel’s — according to the Maharshal, regret does not release.

5. The open machlokot

What does “it is not permitted to him” mean in the Yerushalmi? The Rosh: not of itself, but one releases by regret as for any vow. The Rashba: there is no release at all, such conduct being equal to the prohibitions of the Torah. The seif reports both, and the Rama decides in five words for the first. Beit Yosef · Rama.
Does release by regret have a limit? The Shach, at ס״ק ד, brings from the Maharshal that what all Israel has received is released by no path — and he rests it on the word “others” in the braita. The Ba’er Hetev repeats it. The seif does not say it. Shach ס״ק ד · Ba’er Hetev ס״ק ג.
Does the מנהג חשוב / מנהג גרוע distinction hold? The Rosh and the Tosafot lay it down; the Bach rejects it, because it has no measure. The Shach reports both and inclines to the first. Shach ס״ק ז · Ba’er Hetev ס״ק ה.
Does one who moves keep the customs of his former town? The seif says no, if he has no intention of returning. The Shach writes תימה, sets Orach Chayim 468 against it, proposes a distinction, writes וצ״ע — then concludes in his own name: one always follows the place where one settles, in both directions. Shach ס״ק ח · Ba’er Hetev ס״ק ו.
Is a son bound by his father’s practices? The Pitchei Teshuva, at ס״ק ה, reports from the Zikhron Yosef that he is not — except for what the son himself practised since coming of age, his parents having accustomed him to it. That, he says, is how “and their offspring” in seif 2 must be read. Pitchei Teshuva ס״ק ה.

7. Golden rules, mnemonic and classic pitfalls

The golden rules. (1) What binds is knowing conduct, with the intention to persist. (2) A single occasion suffices. (3) Three men are needed, and one opens with regret. (4) Two sentences said at the outset avoid all of it. (5) And where there is fear of dispute, nothing is changed.
ידע + לעולם → vow · טעות → nothing · כל ישראל → no חרטה · מחלוקת → change nothing
Pitfall 1 — believing one must have repeated it for long. The seif says the opposite: ונהג כן אפילו פעם אחת. What binds is not elapsed time but the intention at the beginning.
Pitfall 2 — believing a serious motive suffices to release. Health is the very example of the seif, and it does not release of itself: three men are needed. The Pitchei Teshuva does open a door at ס״ק א, but it is narrow and aims at a suspension, not an abandonment.
Pitfall 3 — applying the siman to a custom of all Israel. The Shach, at ס״ק ד, excludes precisely that case: release by regret holds only for what a part has taken up.
