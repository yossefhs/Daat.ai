1. The principle: a vow in the service of study

The first seif describes a father who deprives himself of his son’s service so that the son is not torn from study. It is not a punishment, it is a protection — and it is the only place in the tractate where a vow is put to that use.
But the reading is not unanimous. The gemara says only המדיר בנו לתלמוד תורה. The Meḥaber follows the Ramban and the Rosh — the father deprived himself. The Rambam reads the reverse — the father imposed the vow on his son because he was not studying. The Shakh and the Taz infer that in the Rambam’s case even the small services would be forbidden.

2. The four mechanisms

Seif א — presumption: what the father did not mean to aim at (smallness, impossibility)
▼
Seif ב — duration: the vow dies with the father, unless expressly extended
▼
Seif ג — acquisition: קני על מנת להקנות, and what “permitted” means
▼
Seif ד — exit: giving, paying, informing — and לכתחילה against בדיעבד

3. What the apparatus adds, seif by seif

Seif א — Shakh 1 and Taz 1: the reason is smallness, and it does not hold in the other case. Shakh 2: the אנן סהדי of the market. Seif ג — Shakh 3: three readings of the word “מותר”. Taz 2: the mechanism via the סודר. Taz 3: why the Rambam forbids the non-scholarly grandson. Seif ד — Shakh 4: the Ran, the Beit Yosef, the Maharshal, the Prisha. Shakh 5: which debt is meant. Taz 4: who the brothers and sons are. Taz 5: the defence of the Tur. Nekudot HaKesef: “said before him”.

5. The open disputes

Four disagreements run through the siman. None is closed in the text of the Shulḥan Arukh.

Who is the מדיר in seif א? The Ramban and the Rosh — the father deprived himself, and that is what the Meḥaber adopts. The Rambam — the father imposed the vow on his son. The Shakh and the Taz infer that in the Rambam’s case even the small services would be forbidden, and the Ba’ḥ rules so.
What does “מותר” mean in seif ג? The Ra’avad and the Ba’ḥ: it implies that the non-scholarly grandson is forbidden. The Kesef Mishneh and the Perisha: “permitted” means acquired, and he is permitted in any event. The Rosh and the Ran: he acquired nothing at all, and yet he is permitted.
How does the forbidden heir rid himself of the property? The Ran: he shows the place, he does not give. The Beit Yosef at siman 216: an actual gift to the sons and brothers is permitted. The Maharshal gives a complete order, and excludes the heir’s own sons.
לכתחילה or בדיעבד? The Prisha and the Ba’ḥ: only after the fact. The Taz arrives there too at the end of his fifth note. And the Nekudot HaKesef recall that the Drisha and the Ba’ḥ anticipated the Taz.

קני על מנת להקנותto acquire for the sole purpose of conveying
סודרthe cloth of the kinyan — Rav Naḥman’s proof that “acquiring in order to convey” works
טובת הנאהthe moral benefit of being the one who gives
מראה להם מקום“he shows them the place” — the Ran’s solution in seif ד
תוך כדי דיבורwithin the time of an utterance — the Taz’s argument for attaching the condition to the prohibition

7. Recapitulation

ק for קטן: what is small was not aimed at. — ז for זמן: how long does the vow reach? — מ for מראה מקום: one does not give, one shows.
Golden rules. (1) Always establish first who the נאסר is — the father or the son. (2) The criterion of seif א is not the size of the service but its cost in study time. (3) Seven words — “in my lifetime and after my death” — carry the vow across death. (4) In seif ד, the heir does nothing himself if he can avoid it, and he always informs.
Pitfalls. (1) Reading seif א as a father punishing his son: that is the Rambam’s reading, not the Meḥaber’s. (2) Extending the permission of the three services to the reverse case — the Shakh, the Taz and the Ba’ḥ say the opposite. (3) Thinking that “ובן הבן מותר” means one thing: it means three. (4) Reading seif ד as an authorisation to act: the Prisha and the Ba’ḥ see in it a statement after the fact. (5) Attributing to the Taz the answer of seif ד: the Nekudot HaKesef recall that the Drisha and the Ba’ḥ anticipated him.

The line of ruling

The father who forbade himself his son’s service to protect his study remains permitted in trifles — the jar, the lamp, the small fish — and in the market purchase if he is not a man who goes there. If the vow is of the other kind, the Shakh, the Taz and the Ba’ḥ hold that even that is forbidden. The vow lapses at the father’s death, unless expressly extended. And the forbidden heir does nothing with his own hand if he can avoid it: he shows the place, he informs, and — according to the Prisha and the Ba’ḥ — what seif ד describes is permitted after the fact rather than from the outset. A real case goes to a Rav.
