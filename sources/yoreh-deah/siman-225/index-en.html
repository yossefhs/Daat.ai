האוסר על חבירו דבר שאינו שלו

Siman 225 — forbidding another what is not yours, and the word that changes everything (יורה דעה רכ״ה)

A single seif, eighteen words, and a rule that holds up the tractate: my word has purchase only on what is mine. Telling your fellow that a third party’s produce is forbidden to him produces nothing at all — not “it is permitted” but אינו כלום, nothing happened. And it all turns over on one word, which is not in the mouth of the speaker but of the man he targets: amen. Siman 209 is its exact counterpart. (Shulḥan Arukh, Yoreh De’ah 225 — 1 seif; this edition gives it no heading at all.)

האומר לחבירו פירותיו של פלוני אסורין עליך אינו כלום אלא אם כן ענה הנאסר אמן:

One who says to his fellow: so-and-so’s produce is forbidden to you — it is nothing at all, unless the one targeted answered amen. The whole siman is there. The first half annuls; the second reopens, and it places the key in a mouth other than the one that spoke.

Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 225:1

Frequently asked questions — Siman 225

Does this siman really have a title?

No. It is the only one in the block that carries none: its first segment begins directly with « ובו סעיף אחד: » (שו״ע יו״ד רכ״ה:א), where the other simanim open with a formula “דין … ובו … סעיפים”. The English title of these pages, and the Hebrew descriptor beside it, are our own; they describe the seif, they do not reproduce a heading of the Shulḥan Arukh.

If my neighbour declares that something is forbidden to me, am I bound?

It depends on one question: whose is that thing? If it is his, see siman 209 — the vow takes hold, and no acceptance on your part is required. If it belongs to a third party, this is our siman, and his word produced no effect — unless you answered amen.

Is “אינו כלום” the same as “it is permitted”?

No, and the nuance is practical. “Permitted” assumes a prohibition could have existed and was lifted. “אינו כלום” says no prohibition ever took hold. The direct consequence: there is nothing to undo, and one must not go looking for a התרת נדרים for a vow that never happened.

Must one have said the word “amen” exactly?

Siman 209 expressly broadens it: what is covered is “any word whose sense is like answering amen, that is, an acceptance” — « או דבר שעניינו כענית אמן שהוא קבלת דברים » (שו״ע יו״ד ר״ט:א). Whether what you answered falls into that category is a question of fact and of wording. It goes to a Rav.

And the oaths printed at the head of certain books, which nobody accepts?

That is precisely the Pitḥei Teshuva’s question, concerning the adjuration the Rambam placed at the beginning of the Moreh Nevukhim. He reports from the Shtei HaLeḥem that, despite the word used, it is not an oath but a decree of חרם ונידוי, which asks for no amen. And he concludes that it is forbidden to make light of such adjurations.

Why does the Taz write nothing here?

The siman runs to a line and a half, and the Shakh says it all in six words. Nothing about the halakha is to be inferred. The Taz is present in force right next door: five entries at siman 223, three at siman 224 — one of them very long on דין חלוקה. No entry has been manufactured here to complete a table.
