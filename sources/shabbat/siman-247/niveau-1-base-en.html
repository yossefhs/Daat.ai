1. The text of the Shulchan Aruch

Siman רמ״ז contains 6 seifim of the Mehaber (Rabbi Yosef Karo) which specify the conditions under which a Jew may entrust a letter or object to a non-Jewish carrier before Shabbat.

Seif Alef — The general rule

שׁוֹלֵחַ אָדָם אִגֶּרֶת בְּיַד עַכּוּ״ם, וַאֲפִלּוּ בְּעֶרֶב שַׁבָּת עִם חֲשֵׁכָה. וּבִלְבַד שֶׁיִּקְצוֹץ לוֹ דָּמִים, וְשֶׁיֵּצֵא מִפֶּתַח בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל קוֹדֶם הַשַּׁבָּת. וַאֲפִלּוּ קָצַץ, אִם לֹא קָבוּעַ בִּי דּוֹאַר בְּאוֹתָהּ הָעִיר אָסוּר אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן יֵשׁ שָׁהוּת לְהַגִּיעַ קוֹדֶם הַשַּׁבָּת לַבַּיִת הָרִאשׁוֹן הַסָּמוּךְ לַחוֹמָה. וְיֵשׁ מַתִּירִין בְּכָל גַּוְנָא מִיּוֹם רִאשׁוֹן וְעַד יוֹם רְבִיעִי, וַאֲפִלּוּ לֹא קָבוּעַ בִּי דּוֹאַר.
A person may send a letter via a non-Jew — even on Erev Shabbat at nightfall. Provided (1) that he is mekatzetz a price with him (ketzitzah), and (2) that the non-Jew leaves the Jew's doorway before Shabbat comes in.

And even if he was mekatzetz a price: if the bei doar (collecting agent) is not kavua (fixed) in that city, it is assur — unless there is enough time for the non-Jew to arrive before Shabbat at the first house adjacent to the wall of the destination city. And there are those who are matir in all cases from Sunday through Wednesday, even if the bei doar is not kavua.
The central idea of Seif Alef: sending a letter via a non-Jew is fundamentally permitted, but under three cumulative conditions — ketzitzah (price agreed upon), keviut (fixed recipient), and time margin (completed before Shabbat). The Rama relaxes the keviut requirement from Sunday through Wednesday.

The siman revolves around one structural question: when the non-Jew transports the letter on Shabbat, is he doing so as the Jew's agent (shelucho shel Yisrael) or for his own interest (adata de-nafsheh)? The 6 seifim apply this test to 6 borderline cases.

2. The general context

What is this siman about?

Our siman deals with a very concrete situation: a Jew wants to send a letter, package, or merchandise via a non-Jew (typically a carrier, a postal worker, a delivery person). The sending takes place before Shabbat, but the transport will cross Shabbat — or even arrive on Shabbat itself.

The fundamental question: can we permit this, or does it violate the issur of amirah le-akum (asking a non-Jew to do melacha for us on Shabbat)?

Connection with the previous siman (רמ״ו)

Siman רמ״ו dealt with lending and renting objects to a non-Jew for Shabbat. There we were in a question of shevitat kelim (the rest of utensils) and sechar Shabbat (Shabbat compensation).

Our siman רמ״ז addresses another angle: no longer the rental of an object to use, but the sending of an object to be transported. The question is no longer "the non-Jew is causing my utensil to work on Shabbat" but "the non-Jew himself is performing melacha on Shabbat with my object on his back".

The two simanim touch the same nerve: when does the non-Jew act as the Jew's agent (= the problem of amirah le-akum)? Siman רמ״ו resolved it through havlaah (inclusion). Our siman רמ״ז resolves it through ketzitzah (a price agreed upon for the task).

The fundamental question

The issur of amirah le-akum is mi-derabbanan. Three reasons are given by the Rishonim:

SourceReason for the issur
Mechilta (on Yitro)The pasuk "כל מלאכה לא תעשה" (Shemot 20:10) — including via an agent
Rashi (Avodah Zarah 15a)The principle of shelucho shel adam ke-moto (the agent is like the sender) applied to the non-Jew
Rambam (Hil. Shabbat 6:1)So that Shabbat not become light in the eyes of the Jew (kalut Shabbat be-einav)
The conceptual structure of the siman: as soon as the non-Jew visibly acts on behalf of the Jew, we are in the issur of amirah. But if he acts for his own interest (because he is paid per task, or he is making his trip anyway), he leaves the realm of agency and the sending becomes mutar.

3. First key concept — קציצה (the agreed-upon price)

What is ketzitzah?

קציצה (from the root קצץ, "to cut, to fix"): this is the act of agreeing upon a specific price with the non-Jew for the task to be performed, before he begins. Once this price is fixed, the non-Jew is no longer acting in a logic of service to the Jew — he acts to collect his payment.

Why ketzitzah changes everything

With a firm ketzitzah:

  • The non-Jew knows he will be paid a fixed amount, regardless of when he delivers
  • He therefore has an interest in delivering quickly to move on to another customer
  • If he delivers on Shabbat, it is not because the Jew pushed him to do so — it is his own logistical choice
  • Therefore the outside observer does not see the Jew behind the Shabbat melacha

Without ketzitzah:

  • The non-Jew has no specific constraint
  • If he hurries to deliver on Shabbat, it is to please the Jew
  • He visibly becomes the Jew's agent
  • The issur of amirah le-akum applies
"בְּעִינַן דַּוְקָא שֶׁיִּקְצוֹב לוֹ דָּמִים בְּעַד מְלַאכְתּוֹ, דְּאָז אַדַּעְתֵּיהּ דְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ קָעָבִיד וְלֹא בִּשְׁבִיל יִשְׂרָאֵל" (משנה ברורה רמ״ז ס״ק א׳)
Mishnah Berurah (1): We specifically require that he be mekatzetz a price for his work — for then he acts adata de-nafsheh (for his own interest) and not for the Jew.
To verify that the ketzitzah is valid:
  1. Is the price fixed in advance? ✓
  2. Is it independent of the timing of delivery? ✓
  3. Is it certain (not a "we'll see")? ✓
If yes to all three → ketzitzah is valid → the sending falls into the permissive category.

4. Second key concept — אדעתא דנפשיה

What is adata de-nafsheh?

אדעתא דנפשיה (literally "according to his own intention"): this is the halachic test of appearance. The question: when one observes the non-Jew performing his work on Shabbat, is he visibly serving a Jew, or is he visibly acting for his own interest?

The practical test

To apply the test, we ask:

CriterionIf YESIf NO
Does the non-Jew have his own interest in performing the task?Adata de-nafsheh — mutarService to the Jew — assur

"אַדַּעְתֵּיהּ דְּנַפְשֵׁיהּ קָעָבִיד בִּשְׁבִיל שְׂכִירוּתֵיהּ" (משנה ברורה רמ״ז ס״ק ג׳)
Mishnah Berurah (3): He acts adata de-nafsheh (for his own interest), because of his payment.
Adata de-nafsheh is the consequence of the ketzitzah. Ketzitzah is the objective mechanism (the price contract); adata de-nafsheh is the visible effect (the non-Jew appears to act for himself). The two are linked: without ketzitzah, no adata; with a firm ketzitzah, the adata is realized.

5. Third key concept — קביעות בי דואר

What is keviut bei doar?

בי דואר (literally "house of the post") = a professional collecting agent in the destination city, who receives all the letters to redistribute them.
קביעות = that he be fixed and established permanently in that location.

Why this condition? The Mehaber is choshesh that if the recipient is not findable on arrival, the non-Jew will be obliged to chase after him on Shabbat — and there, the additional melacha is clearly caused by the Jew's mission.

Modern application

The modern equivalent of בי דואר:
  • 🏢 The central post office of a city (always open during standard hours)
  • 🏬 An Amazon hub or shipping company (structural collection point)
  • 🏠 A recipient who doesn't move — a stable residential address
Conversely: if the non-Jew has to deliver "by hand" to a mobile person (uncertain residence), it is less permissive.

The Rama's position

The Rama brings a more lenient opinion: from Sunday through Wednesday, sending is permitted even if the bei doar is not kavua. The logic: we are far from Shabbat, so the observer does not make the connection between the sending and a Shabbat melacha.

Practically:
  • 📅 Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday → one can send even without keviut (according to the Rama)
  • 📅 Thursday, Friday → one needs ketzitzah and keviut, or a sufficient time margin so that the delivery is completed before Shabbat

6. The 6 seifim in detail — one by one

Seif Alef — The general framework

Let us review in detail. The Mehaber permits sending a letter via a non-Jew, even on Erev Shabbat at nightfall, under three conditions:

  1. Ketzitzah — a price agreed upon in advance
  2. Departure before Shabbat — the non-Jew leaves the Jew's house before Shabbat comes in (knisat ha-Shabbat)
  3. Either keviut bei doar, or a time margin — either the recipient is a fixed collecting agent, or the non-Jew has time to arrive before Shabbat at the "first house adjacent to the wall" (= the entrance to the destination city)
The Mehaber's key phrase: "וַאֲפִלּוּ קָצַץ, אִם לֹא קָבוּעַ בִּי דּוֹאַר בְּאוֹתָהּ הָעִיר אָסוּר" — even with ketzitzah, if there is no keviut → assur. The ketzitzah alone is therefore not sufficient if the delivery will spill over Shabbat in an uncertain manner.

Seif Bet — Implicit ketzitzah

וְאִם הִתְנָה עִמּוֹ שֶׁיִּתֵּן לוֹ שְׂכָרוֹ אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁלֹּא פֵּרֵשׁ כַּמָּה יִתֵּן לוֹ דִּינוֹ כְּקוֹצֵץ, דְּסַמְכֵיהּ דַּעְתֵּיהּ דְּעַכּוּ״ם וּבְדִידֵיהּ קָא טָרַח. אֲבָל בִּסְתָם אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁיֵּשׁ בְּדַעְתּוֹ שֶׁיִּתֵּן לוֹ שָׂכָר אָסוּר דְּלֹא סַמְכֵיהּ דַּעְתֵּיהּ וּבְיִשְׂרָאֵל קָא טָרַח.
If one stipulated with him that he would give him his wages, even without specifying how much — this is considered ketzitzah. For the non-Jew relies on the payment (without knowing the exact amount) and works for himself. But in setam (default, with no agreement), even if one has the intention to pay him, it is assur — for he does not rely on the payment and works for the Jew.
Critical distinction: what matters is not the exact amount, but the existence of a firm agreement. A firm promise without a figure = OK. An uncommunicated intention = problem.

Seif Gimel — The daily-wage worker

אִם שְׂכָרוֹ לְיָמִים דָּבָר קָצוּב בְּכָל יוֹם בַּהֲלִיכָתוֹ וּבַחֲזִירָתוֹ, אֶלָּא שֶׁאֵינוֹ מַקְפִּיד עִמּוֹ מָתַי יֵלֵךְ — אִם הוּא בְּעֶרֶב שַׁבָּת אָסוּר, דִּכְשֶׁיּוֹצֵא בְּשַׁבָּת נִרְאֶה כְּאִלּוּ הִתְנָה עִמּוֹ כָּךְ.
If one hired him by the day with a fixed wage per day, without worrying about the exact timing — on Friday it is assur, because when he leaves on Shabbat it appears as if one had stipulated this with him.

Why is Friday specifically targeted? Because the immediate proximity of Shabbat causes the observer to make the connection: "he hired him yesterday evening, he goes out today on Shabbat — he must have asked him for Shabbat". On Tuesday or Wednesday, this connection falls away.

Seif Daled — Free transport

הָעַכּוּ״ם מוֹלִיךְ הַכְּתָב בְּחִנָּם, אֲפִלּוּ נְתָנָהּ לוֹ בְּעֶרֶב שַׁבָּת מֻתָּר, שֶׁהֲרֵי הָעַכּוּ״ם מֵאֵלָיו הוּא עוֹשֶׂה זֶה וְאֵינוֹ אֶלָּא לְהַחֲזִיק טוֹבָה לְיִשְׂרָאֵל מִפְּנֵי מַה שֶׁקִּיבֵּל מִמֶּנּוּ. הגה: וְיֵשׁ חוֹלְקִים וּסְבִירָא לְהוּ דְּכָל שֶׁעוֹשֶׂה בְּחִנָּם אָסוּר, וְטוֹב לְהַחְמִיר.
If the non-Jew transports the letter for free — even if it was given to him on Erev Shabbat, it is mutar. For he acts on his own (out of gratitude for a prior service from the Jew). Gloss of the Rama: Some hold to the contrary that any free transport is assur (without payment, the non-Jew has no personal interest, so he acts for the Jew). And it is good to be machmir.
Practical consequence: according to the Rama (followed in Ashkenazi practice), one should at least promise a small symbolic payment to the non-Jewish carrier, even if he proposes to do it for free. A bottle of water, a tip — something that constitutes a minimal ketzitzah.

Seif Hei — The non-Jew is going on his own

אִם הָעַכּוּ״ם הוֹלֵךְ מֵעַצְמוֹ לְמָקוֹם אַחֵר וְיִשְׂרָאֵל נוֹתֵן לוֹ אִגֶּרֶת — מֻתָּר בְּכָל גַּוְנָא.
If the non-Jew is going on his own to another place, and the Jew gives him a letter to carry along the way — mutar in all cases.
This is the most permissive case in the siman. The non-Jew is making his own trip; he takes the letter in passing. No need for ketzitzah, no need for keviut, no need for a time margin. The non-Jew is clearly acting for himself.

Reservation: the Beis Yosef clarifies (cited by the MB) that if one knows the non-Jew is making a detour for the Jew, this is no longer mutar — the detour reintroduces service to the Jew.

Seif Vav — Yearly employee

מִי שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ שָׂכִיר עַכּוּ״ם לְשָׁנָה אוֹ יוֹתֵר — אָסוּר לְשַׁלְּחוֹ עֶרֶב שַׁבָּת בְּאִגֶּרֶת. הגה: וּמִיהוּ אִם לֹא שְׂכָרוֹ רַק בִּשְׁלִיחוּת אִגֶּרֶת יֵשׁ מַתִּירִין, כְּמוֹ שֶׁנִּתְבָּאֵר לְעֵיל סִי' רמ"ד ס"ה.
One who has a non-Jewish employee hired by the year (or more) — it is assur to send him on Friday with a letter. Gloss of the Rama: However, if one hired him specifically for letter-carrying service, there are matirin (cf. Siman 244, Seif 5).
The problem of the yearly employee: his salary is not tied to this specific task — it is a global amount. So even if one tells him "carry this letter", there is no ketzitzah on this task: he acts as a general employee, and so visibly works for the Jew.

7. The Rama's position — what changes for an Ashkenazi

The Rama (Rabbi Moshe Isserles) adds three important leniencies for Ashkenazi practice:

Leniency 1 — From Sunday through Wednesday without keviut

The Rama writes explicitly: "וְיֵשׁ מַתִּירִין בְּכָל גַּוְנָא מִיּוֹם רִאשׁוֹן וְעַד יוֹם רְבִיעִי, וַאֲפִלּוּ לֹא קָבוּעַ בִּי דּוֹאַר". From Sunday through Wednesday, one is matir sending a letter via a non-Jew even without keviut bei doar.

The logic: we are far enough from Shabbat that no one will make the connection between the sending and a Shabbat melacha. The risk of "nireh ke-shelucho" (appearance of agency, i.e. maaras ayin) falls away.

Leniency 2 — Recommendation of ketzitzah even for free transport

As we saw in Seif Daled, the Rama says "ve-tov le-hachmir" — it is good to be machmir and always agree on a small payment, even if the non-Jew proposes to transport for free.

Leniency 3 — Employee hired specifically for letter-carrying

As we saw in Seif Vav, if one hired the non-Jew specifically for letter-carrying service (and not as a general employee), the Rama brings a lenient opinion by referring to Siman 244.

8. Modern practical cases

Case 1 — Postal service (standard letter)

Case: I send an ordinary letter to a recipient on Friday morning.
Analysis:
  • Ketzitzah ✓ (the rate is set in advance by the post)
  • Keviut ✓ (central post offices are fixed bei doar)
  • Departure before Shabbat ✓ (the letter leaves my house Friday morning)
Conduct: mutar according to both the Mehaber and the Rama.

Case 2 — Amazon Prime delivery Friday for Saturday

Case: I order a product on Thursday with Prime delivery "Saturday before noon".
Analysis:
  • Ketzitzah ✓ (delivery fees set at the time of order)
  • But! The delivery arrives on Shabbat itself
  • And it is a material package, not just a letter — the receipt itself may pose a problem
Conduct:
  • The sending on Thursday is technically mutar (ketzitzah + sufficient delay for Friday delivery)
  • But receiving a package on Shabbat is another question (Siman 252 — not our topic here)
  • Better to schedule delivery outside of Shabbat to avoid the cascading issue

Case 3 — Express delivery Friday for Friday evening

Case: I send a package Friday morning with express delivery "Friday delivery before 6 PM".
Analysis:
  • Ketzitzah ✓ (fixed rate)
  • Keviut ✓ (shipping companies have permanent sorting hubs)
  • Time margin: conceptually possible for it to arrive before Shabbat
Conduct: mutar. Even if in practice the package were to overflow slightly, the test of "mi-be'od yom" (enough time before Shabbat at origin) is fulfilled.

Case 4 — Independent neighbor offering for free

Case: a non-Jewish neighbor tells me "I'm passing through Marseille tomorrow, I can carry your package for free".
Analysis:
  • Mehaber: mutar (adata de-nafsheh — he is making his own trip)
  • Rama: it is good to offer a small symbolic payment
Conduct (Ashkenazi): give a bottle of water or 5 € symbolically to convert it into ketzitzah.

Case 5 — Email scheduled for Shabbat

Case: I write an email Friday morning and schedule it for sending on Shabbat at 2 PM.
Analysis:
  • The sending is automatic (machine, not a human non-Jew)
  • No direct amirah le-akum
  • But the recipient will see it on Shabbat, and some poskim are choshesh about visible Shabbat activity
Conduct: a broader topic than our siman (also touches on automatic melacha). In practice, most contemporary poskim permit a scheduled email as long as the content is not for Shabbat use.

9. Practical summary of the Siman

The 3 cumulative conditions for sending via a non-Jew:
  1. Ketzitzah — a price agreed upon in advance (a shipping rate, an explicit promise, etc.)
  2. Departure before Shabbat — the non-Jew leaves the Jew's house before Shabbat comes in
  3. Either keviut (a fixed collecting agent at the destination) or time margin (enough time to deliver before Shabbat)

Practical decision table

Situation Conduct
I send a letter Sunday-Tuesday Mutar without concern (ketzitzah + departure before Shabbat suffice; the Rama exempts even from keviut)
I send a letter Wednesday-Thursday Mutar with ketzitzah + keviut, or sufficient time margin
I send a letter Friday before Shabbat Mutar only with the 3 strict conditions (firm ketzitzah + keviut + margin)
I use postal/shipping service Mutar — the rate is fixed, hubs are fixed, it is a professional service
A non-Jewish neighbor offers to carry for free Mutar according to Mehaber; according to Rama, give a symbolic payment
My non-Jewish (yearly) employee — sending him on Friday Assur, unless hired specifically for letter-carrying
The non-Jew is going on his own to that destination Mutar in all cases (except detour for the Jew)

The 5 practical principles of Siman רמ״ז

  1. Always agree on a price in advance with the non-Jewish carrier (ketzitzah, be-kablanut)
  2. Make sure the non-Jew leaves your house before Shabbat (knisat ha-Shabbat)
  3. Favor professional services (postal, shipping) over informal arrangements
  4. Do not send a permanent employee on Friday — use an external service
  5. Do not explicitly ask the non-Jew to deliver on Shabbat (direct amirah = absolute issur)
